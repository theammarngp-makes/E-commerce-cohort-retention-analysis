# 12. Business Insights

## Insight 1 - Repeat purchasing is structurally rare, not just "low"
**Observation:** Average month-1 retention across mature cohorts (Jan-Jun 2017) is 0.33%, and no single cohort-month combination in the dataset exceeds 0.65%.
**Evidence:** Cohort retention matrix, `month_number = 1` across all 19 cohorts with available data; best case is the Aug 2017 cohort at 0.65%, worst is Jul 2018 at 0.099%.
**Why it matters:** This is not a normal "retention curve" pattern where a meaningful share of customers stick around and a long tail churns. Here, virtually the entire customer base is one-and-done. Any retention strategy has to start from acquisition/onboarding, not from optimizing an existing repeat-purchase funnel — because that funnel barely exists yet.

## Insight 2 - Retention doesn't meaningfully improve or worsen over the observed period
**Observation:** Month-1 retention fluctuates between 0.17% (Feb 2017) and 0.65% (Aug 2017) with no clear upward trend across 2017-2018, even as cohort size (i.e., acquisition volume) grew roughly 8x.
**Evidence:** Month-1 retention by cohort_month, Jan 2017 - Jul 2018.
**Why it matters:** Scaling acquisition did not organically fix retention — if anything, later cohorts (2018) show similar or slightly lower month-1 retention than the best 2017 cohorts. This suggests retention needs a deliberate intervention; it will not improve on its own as the business grows.

## Insight 3 - Whatever repeat purchases do happen are front-loaded, then long-tail low
**Observation:** Average retention across mature cohorts is highest in months 1-2 (~0.33-0.36%), dips through months 3-8 (0.20-0.32%), and drops further by month 13+ (~0.15%), with occasional single-digit-customer upticks likely reflecting small sample noise rather than genuine re-engagement waves.
**Evidence:** Average retention curve, months 0-19, mature cohort subset (Jan-Jun 2017).
**Why it matters:** If a re-engagement campaign is going to work, the highest-leverage window is the first 1-2 months after a customer's first order — waiting longer means competing against an already-decayed base.
